Talinghaga at Pahiwatig

Figures of speech, euphemism, and the art of not saying it

At C1 you understood what was said. At C2 you understand what was avoided. Filipino conversation carries an enormous amount of meaning indirectly — through figures, through euphemism, and through remarks aimed sideways — and none of it is announced.

This lesson names the machinery, then trains the reading.

1. Tayutay — the Figures, Named

Filipino has its own terminology for figures of speech, and it is taught in every Filipino class in the country. Knowing the terms is part of the register.

TagalogEnglishExample
pagtutuladsimileParang bituin ang mga mata niya.
pagwawangismetaphorBituin ang mga mata niya.
pagsasataopersonificationUmiiyak ang langit.
pagmamalabishyperboleSandaang taon na akong naghihintay.
balintunairony"Ang ganda ng panahon," sabi niya sa gitna ng bagyo.
pasukdolclimax (rising series)Umiyak, humagulgol, at halos mawalan ng malay.
pagpapalit-tawagmetonymyNakinig ang buong bayan. (= the people)
sagisagsymbolAng kalapati bilang sagisag ng kapayapaan.
Pagtutulad vs pagwawangis. The split is the same as English simile vs metaphor, and the test is the same: if para, parang, or tulad ng is present, it is pagtutulad. If the comparison is asserted outright, it is pagwawangis.

2. Talinghaga Is Bigger Than Metaphor

Talinghaga is often glossed as "metaphor", and that undersells it. It is the layer of hidden meaning a text carries — the thing a tanaga has, that a proverb has, that a careful remark has.

The tanaga from Lesson 11 — Katitibay ka tulos… ako'y mumunting lumot — contains no figure of speech you could label. There is no simile, no personification. There is only a stake and some moss.

And yet the poem is entirely about devotion. That is talinghaga: the whole utterance means something other than what it says, and nothing in it points this out.

3. Pahiwatig — Meaning by Implication

Pahiwatig is a hint; pagpapahiwatig is the act of implying rather than stating. Filipino conversation uses it far more than English does, and refusing to read it is the single most common way a fluent foreigner still misunderstands a room.

What is saidWhat is meant
"Ang aga mo ngayon, ah."You are late. (said to someone who is)
"Sige, tingnan natin."Probably no.
"Baka next time na lang."No.
"Mag-iingat ka sa mga kasama mo."I don't trust one of them specifically.
"Busy ka siguro."You haven't replied and I noticed.

Pasaring — the sideways remark

A pasaring is a pointed comment aimed at someone without naming them. Everyone present understands the target; the speaker retains deniability.

At a family gathering, loudly, to no one: "Mabuti pa ang iba, marunong tumulong." — "Good for those people who know how to help."

On social media: the subtweet is the direct descendant. Filipinos call it a pasaring or parinig.

4. Euphemism

Filipino softens the unbearable systematically. Using the blunt word where a euphemism is expected reads as cold, not as honest.

EuphemismLiteralMeans

pumanawdeparteddied
may dinaramdamis feeling somethingis unwell
nasa maayos na kalagayanis in a settled conditionis doing all right (often: not really)
hindi na kami magkasundowe no longer agreethe relationship ended

5. Why Indirection Is Not Dishonesty

Indirection here protects hiya — the sense of social face you met at B2 and will meet again in Lesson 15. Saying no outright forces the other person to have been refused, in public. A pahiwatig lets both people leave with dignity intact.

From the outside this can look like evasiveness. From the inside, the blunt version is what looks like aggression. Neither culture is being unclear — they are protecting different things. At C2, you are expected to be able to operate in both modes and to choose.

Reading practice. A colleague asks you to take an extra shift. You reply: "Ay, titingnan ko pa, ha? Medyo marami akong ginagawa nitong linggo."

What you have said: I'll see; I'm quite busy this week.

What you have communicated: no — and your colleague now knows not to ask again, without either of you having said the word.

Practice — Reading Between the Lines

Exercise 1: Name the tayutay

  • "Parang bituin ang mga mata niya." → _____ (Answer: pagtutulad)
  • "Bituin ang mga mata niya." → _____ (Answer: pagwawangis)
  • "Umiiyak ang langit." → _____ (Answer: pagsasatao)
  • "Sandaang taon na akong naghihintay." → _____ (Answer: pagmamalabis)

Exercise 2: Give the Filipino term

  • irony → _____ (Answer: balintuna)
  • metonymy → _____ (Answer: pagpapalit-tawag)
  • symbol → _____ (Answer: sagisag)
  • climax (rising series) → _____ (Answer: pasukdol)

Exercise 3: What is actually meant?

Answer yes or no:

  • "Baka next time na lang." → _____ (Answer: no)
  • "Sige, tingnan natin." → probably _____ (Answer: no)
  • "Ay oo naman, sasama ako!" → _____ (Answer: yes)

Exercise 4: Decode the euphemism

  • sumakabilang-buhay → _____ (Answer: died)
  • may dinaramdam → is _____ (Answer: unwell|ill|sick)
  • hindi na kami magkasundo → the relationship _____ (Answer: ended)

Exercise 5: Name the move

Answer pahiwatig, pasaring, or talinghaga:

  • A remark aimed at someone present, without naming them → _____ (Answer: pasaring)
  • A poem about moss that is really about devotion → _____ (Answer: talinghaga)
  • "Busy ka siguro," meaning you never replied → _____ (Answer: pahiwatig)

Where This Takes You

You can now read what a Tagalog sentence declines to say. That skill is the foundation of the next one: Lesson 15 asks you to carry meaning across into another language — including the words, like tampo and utang na loob, that carry this whole cultural apparatus inside them.
