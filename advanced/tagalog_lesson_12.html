Tuluyan at Sinaunang Anyo

Thirty forms that unlock a century of Tagalog prose

Open a Tagalog novel from 1920, or a pre-war translation of Rizal, and the page looks foreign in a way that nothing in this course has prepared you for. Apostrophes everywhere. Pronouns you have never seen. A speech tag that is not sabi.

It is the same language. What changed is the spelling and a small set of function words. Learn roughly thirty forms and a century of Tagalog literature opens.

1. The Connectors With Apostrophes

Those apostrophes are contractions — an older written convention for a conjunction plus at ("and"). Modern orthography simply dropped them.

Old formModernMeaning
nguni'tngunitbut
subali'tsubalitbut / however
datapwa't(datapwat) — largely retiredhowever / nevertheless

Two of these are still alive. Ngunit and sapagkat remain fully current in formal writing — you met them at B2. Datapwa't and saka't are genuinely archaic and will mark a text as old the moment you see them.

Kata'y magkasama sa lahat ng bagay. — We two are together in all things. (modern: Tayong dalawa ay magkasama…)

Yaon ang bahay na tinutukoy ko. — That is the house I mean.

The lost dual. Kata is a genuine grammatical category modern Tagalog no longer has. Some varieties — Marinduque especially (Lesson 10) — preserve it. When you meet it in an old text, read it as an intimacy marker: it is used between two people who are close.

3. Literary Speech Tags

Older narrative does not say sabi ni Juan. It says:

FormUseExample
ani + name"said (name)""Tama ka," ani Juan.
aniya"said he / said she""Hindi ako aalis," aniya.
anang + noun"said the…"Anang matanda, malapit na ang bagyo.
wika niya"said he/she" (formal, still used)"Salamat," wika niya.

You met anang already in Lesson 5, as a source marker. It is the same word — old fiction and modern evidentiality drawing on one form.

The rule of thumb for reading old text: c and q are k, final -ao and -ay stay but medial u-glides become w, and the tilde over ng is just ng. Once you apply those three substitutions mentally, most 19th-century Tagalog reads normally.

5. Baybayin

Baybayin is the pre-colonial Philippine script. Its key properties:

  • It is a syllabary, not an alphabet — each character is a consonant plus an inherent a.
  • A mark above or below the character changes the vowel (to i/e or o/u).
  • In its original use it had no way to write a final consonant, which made it ambiguous — a real limitation, not a colonial slander.
  • The Spanish-era kudlit cross (krus-kudlit) was added later to cancel the inherent vowel.
What the revival actually is. Baybayin today is a cultural and artistic practice — signage, tattoos, logos, heritage bills in Congress — not a working orthography. Claims that it was universally used across the archipelago, or that it encodes lost wisdom, outrun the evidence. It was one of several related scripts in the Luzon and Visayas regions, used mostly for letters and verse, and it was replaced because the Latin alphabet wrote final consonants and it did not.

6. Reading an Old Passage

"Nguni't ang pusong yaon ay hindi natinag, sapagka't batid niyang wala nang magagawa. 'Kata'y magkakahiwalay,' aniya, at ang kaniyang tinig ay nanginig."

Substitutions: nguni't → ngunit  ·  yaon → iyon  ·  sapagka't → sapagkat  ·  kata'y → tayong dalawa ay  ·  aniya → sabi niya  ·  kaniyang → kanyang

Modern Tagalog: Ngunit ang pusong iyon ay hindi natinag, sapagkat batid niyang wala nang magagawa. "Tayong dalawa ay magkakahiwalay," sabi niya, at ang kanyang tinig ay nanginig.

English: But that heart was not moved, because she knew nothing more could be done. "We two shall be parted," she said, and her voice trembled.

Where This Takes You

Old prose and old verse are now readable. What is still opaque is the compressed wisdom — the proverbs and riddles that a Tagalog speaker can deploy in six words and expect you to unpack. That is Lesson 13.
