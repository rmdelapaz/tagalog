Tula — Tugma at Sukat

Rhyme, metre, and the classical verse forms of Tagalog

Traditional Tagalog poetry is measured and bound. Every line in a stanza has the same number of syllables, and every line in a stanza rhymes. These are not ornaments a poet may take or leave — they are the form itself, and a reader who cannot hear them is missing most of what the poem is doing.

The good news: the rules are simple, and they are countable.

1. Sukat — Counting Syllables

Sukat is the number of syllables in each line (taludtod). Classical stanzas (saknong) are four lines of equal length.

Counting is straightforward — every vowel is one syllable, and Tagalog has no silent letters:

ka-ti-ti-bay = 4  ·  sa-ka-ling = 3  ·  pu-pu-lu-pot = 4

Watch the glottal-separated vowels: lo-ob is two syllables, not one.

2. Tugma — and Why It Isn't English Rhyme

Classical Tagalog stanzas are monorhyme: all four lines rhyme with each other. But "rhyme" here means something looser and more systematic than in English. Words rhyme when their final syllables end in the same sound class.

ClassEndingsExamples that rhyme
Patinig (vowel)a / e-i / o-ubata — sinta — luha
Malakas (hard consonant)b, k, d, g, p, s, tagos — lumot — liwanag
Mahina (soft consonant)l, m, n, ng, r, w, ymapanglaw — pagitan — silang — masukal
This is why Tagalog verse can rhyme so freely. Masukal and mapanglaw share no final sound at all in English terms — but l and w are both soft consonants, so they rhyme. The system opens up a huge rhyming vocabulary, which is exactly what a 400-stanza narrative poem needs.

3. Reading a Classical Stanza

The opening of Florante at Laura (Francisco Balagtas, 1838) — an awit, twelve syllables to a line:

Sa isang madilim gubat na mapanglaw,
dawag na matinik ay walang pagitan,
halos naghihirap ang kay Febong silang
dumalaw sa loob na lubhang masukal.

Sukat: 12 – 12 – 12 – 12. Count the first line: Sa-i-sang-ma-di-lim-gu-bat-na-ma-pang-law.

Tugma: mapanglaw / pagitan / silang / masukal — w, n, ng, l are all mahina. Monorhyme.

Sense: "In a dark and mournful forest, thorny thickets without a gap, the light of Phoebus can scarcely struggle in to visit that deep tangle."

And a classical tanaga — four lines, seven syllables, one image:

Katitibay, ka tulos,
sakaling datnang agos!
Ako'y mumunting lumot,
sa iyo'y pupulupot.

Sukat: 7 – 7 – 7 – 7. Tugma: tulos / agos / lumot / pulupot — s and t, both malakas.

Sense: "Stand firm, O stake, should the flood come! I am only a little moss — I will wind myself around you."

Talinghaga: the whole poem is a figure for devotion and dependence. The tanaga never states its meaning; that is the form's rule.

The pasyon is the most-heard Tagalog verse in the country. Every Holy Week it is chanted aloud — the pabasa — in homes and chapels, often continuously for days. Millions of Filipinos who have never opened a poetry book know hundreds of stanzas of it by ear.

5. Balagtasan — Argument as Verse

A balagtasan is a staged debate conducted entirely in rhymed, measured verse, with a lakandiwa presiding as judge. It was named for Balagtas and became a popular spectacle in the 1920s, descending from the older courtship-debate game duplo.

It is worth knowing for two reasons. It is where the parallelism and rhetorical questions of Lesson 9 come from, and it is the reason Filipino public argument still carries a performative, rhythmic quality that surprises outsiders.

6. Modern Verse

Malayang taludturan — free verse — abandons both tugma and sukat. In Filipino poetry that is a deliberate, visible break: a reader raised on the pasyon hears the absence of metre as a statement in itself. Contemporary poets move between the two, and the choice is never neutral.

Exercise 1: Count the sukat

Give the number of syllables:

  • "Katitibay, ka tulos" → _____ (Answer: 7)
  • "Sa isang madilim gubat na mapanglaw" → _____ (Answer: 12)
  • "sa iyo'y pupulupot" → _____ (Answer: 7)

Exercise 2: Name the rhyme class

Answer patinig, malakas, or mahina:

  • lumot → _____ (Answer: malakas)
  • mapanglaw → _____ (Answer: mahina)
  • sinta → _____ (Answer: patinig)
  • pagitan → _____ (Answer: mahina)

Exercise 3: Do they rhyme?

Answer oo or hindi by the traditional rule:

  • agos / lumot → _____ (Answer: oo)
  • masukal / silang → _____ (Answer: oo)
  • liwanag / bayan → _____ (Answer: hindi)

Exercise 4: Name the form

  • 12-syllable narrative verse, as in Florante at Laura → _____ (Answer: awit)
  • 8-syllable narrative verse, as in Ibong Adarna → _____ (Answer: korido)
  • 4 lines of 7 syllables, one image → _____ (Answer: tanaga)
  • the chanted Holy Week narrative → _____ (Answer: pasyon)

Exercise 5: Verse vocabulary

  • a single line of verse → _____ (Answer: taludtod)
  • a stanza → _____ (Answer: saknong)
  • a poet → _____ (Answer: makata)
  • a debate in verse → _____ (Answer: balagtasan)

Where This Takes You

You can now open a classical Tagalog poem and hear its machinery. The obstacle that remains is the language of the older texts — nguni't, ani, yaon, and a spelling system that was retired decades ago. Lesson 12 clears that away.
