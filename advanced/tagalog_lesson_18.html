Filipino or Tagalog? — the capstone, and your plan from here

Eighteen lessons ago this course promised to finish the grammar and then take you where the language keeps its memory. That is done. This last lesson steps back from using Tagalog to asking what it is — a question Filipinos themselves argue about, and one you will eventually be asked to have a view on.

Then it makes a plan, because a C2 that is not maintained is a C1 within two years.

1. Filipino or Tagalog?

These are not two names for the same thing, and the difference is constitutional.

Why this is a live argument. Speakers of Cebuano, Ilocano, Hiligaynon and others point out that "Filipino enriched by all Philippine languages" has in practice meant Tagalog with some loanwords — and that a national language which is one region's mother tongue advantages that region. Tagalog speakers point to the practical need for a common language. Both positions are held sincerely by serious people.

As a learner, the useful posture is accuracy and humility: say Tagalog when you mean the language you studied, Filipino when you mean the national standard, and do not tell a Cebuano speaker their language is a dialect.

2. Language in the Schools

Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE) taught the early grades in the local language, on the argument that children learn to read fastest in the language they already speak. It was institutionalised by the Enhanced Basic Education Act of 2013.

It has been contested throughout — over materials, teacher preparation, and results — and in 2024 a law was passed discontinuing the mother tongue as the medium of instruction in kindergarten through Grade 3, except in areas that opt to retain it. The underlying question is not settled, and educators remain divided.

Why an advanced learner should know this. It comes up. Language policy is a normal topic of Filipino public conversation in a way it is not in many countries, and having a rough grasp of the timeline lets you follow the discussion rather than nod through it.

3. The Slang Layers

Slang is not one thing. Filipino has at least four distinguishable layers, and they entered by different routes.

LayerHow it worksExamples
BalbalGeneral street slangastig (tough, from tigas), chibog (food)
Syllable reversalReverse the syllableslodi ← idol, petmalu ← malupit, werpa ← power, dehins ← hindi, repapips ← pare
Swardspeak / bekiFrom the gay community; elaborate substitution and playchika, jowa, keri, charot, bongga
Internet / Gen ZFast-moving, meme-drivensana all, charot, G (= game, "I'm in")
Swardspeak has shaped mainstream Filipino more than any other slang source. Chika, jowa, bongga, and charot all originated in gay speech and are now used by everyone, usually by speakers with no idea where they came from. It is one of the clearest cases anywhere of a marginalised community's language becoming the national vernacular.

A practical note: slang dates fast, and using last decade's slang marks you more visibly than using none. Lodi and petmalu already read as slightly dated. Learn to recognise these layers; be conservative about deploying them.

4. Diaspora Tagalog

Roughly ten million Filipinos live abroad, and the Tagalog spoken in those communities is a variety in its own right:

  • Conservative in places — forms and slang frozen at the year of departure.
  • Heavily code-switched — with English, Arabic, Japanese, Italian, depending on where.
  • Simplified across generations — second-generation speakers often have strong comprehension and reduced production, a normal heritage-speaker profile.

If you learned your Tagalog from a family abroad, this is probably the variety you speak — and it is a real one, not a defective version of Manila Tagalog.

5. Keeping It — the Maintenance Plan

C2 is not a destination; it is a condition that decays without use. What actually maintains an advanced language:

HabitWhy it worksConcretely
Read something real weeklyKeeps the registers of Lessons 7–9 liveA Filipino news section, an essay, a chapter of a novel
Listen to unscripted speechScripted media trains only one puntoRegional vlogs, podcasts, radio call-ins
Write and get correctedProduction decays faster than comprehensionA journal entry weekly; a language partner monthly
Keep one register uncomfortableComfort is where growth stopsTry a talumpati, a translation, a poem
Review deliberatelyAdvanced vocabulary is low-frequency by natureThe spaced-repetition deck in this course

A realistic weekly minimum: one real text read, one unscripted thing listened to, one paragraph written. Thirty minutes total. That holds C2. Anything more grows it.

6. Where You Are Now

Across three courses you have covered the ground: the sound system and core grammar at A1–A2; the focus families, complex sentences, register and discourse at B1–B2; and at C1–C2 the complete focus inventory, the word-building rules, formal syntax, evidentiality, the deep and specialist registers, verse, classical prose, proverbs, figurative depth, translation, editing, and oratory.

What remains is not on any syllabus. It is hours — with real texts, real speakers, and the patience to keep reaching one register past comfortable. Kung may tiyaga, may nilaga.
