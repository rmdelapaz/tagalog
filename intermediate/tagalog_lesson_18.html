Mga Sawikain at Kasabihan — Idioms & Figurative Language

When Filipinos don't mean it literally: idioms, metaphors, and proverbs

Real Tagalog is full of figurative language — phrases whose meaning you can't get by adding up the words. A stubborn person is "hard-headed"; a generous one has "an open palm." These sawikain (idioms) and kasabihan (proverbs) carry culture and warmth, and understanding them is what lets you follow jokes, songs, elders, and everyday teasing.

1. Literal vs. Figurative

An idiom's words point one way; its meaning points another. Take matigas ang ulo:

  • Literal: "hard is the head" — the head is physically hard.
  • Figurative (real meaning): stubborn. "Matigas ang ulo ng bata" = The child is stubborn.

You can't translate word-by-word — you learn the whole phrase as a unit, exactly like English "piece of cake" or "cold feet."

2. Body-Part Idioms

Many Tagalog idioms build on the body:

IdiomLiterallyMeans
matigas ang ulohard headstubborn
balat-sibuyasonion-skinnedoverly sensitive, easily hurt
bukas ang paladopen palmgenerous
mababaw ang luhashallow tearscries easily
malikot ang kamayrestless handslight-fingered / a thief
makati ang paaitchy feetloves to wander / gad about
  • Huwag kang balat-sibuyas — biro lang 'yun. — Don't be so sensitive — it was just a joke.
  • Bukas ang palad ng lola ko sa mahihirap. — My grandma is generous to the poor.

3. Home & Family Idioms

Others center on the home and relationships — a window into Filipino values:

IdiomLiterallyMeans
haligi ng tahananpillar of the homethe father (provider/support)
ilaw ng tahananlight of the homethe mother
kabiyak ng dibdibthe other half of the chestone's spouse
namamangka sa dalawang ilogrowing in two riverstwo-timing
Idioms carry values. Calling the father the haligi (pillar) and the mother the ilaw (light) of the home reflects traditional Filipino family roles — the structure and the warmth. Even when family shapes change, these phrases live on with affection.

4. Everyday Sayings (Kasabihan)

Kasabihan are proverbs — compact wisdom passed down for generations:

ProverbMeaning
Kung may tiyaga, may nilaga.If there's perseverance, there's stew — hard work pays off.
Aanhin pa ang damo kung patay na ang kabayo?What use is the grass if the horse is dead? — too little, too late.
Ang hindi marunong lumingon sa pinanggalingan……ay hindi makararating sa paroroonan — one who forgets their roots won't get far.

5. Using Idioms Naturally

Idioms are seasoning, not the whole meal. A few guidelines:

  • Recognize first. Your first goal is understanding them when others speak — in teasing, songs, and elders' advice.
  • Use sparingly. Drop one where it fits; over-stuffing sounds forced.
  • Mind the register. Idioms are usually casual/warm; proverbs can be weighty. Match Lesson 13's register sense.

Where This Takes You

Figurative Tagalog — sawikain (idioms like matigas ang ulo, bukas ang palad) and kasabihan (proverbs like kung may tiyaga, may nilaga) — is where the language keeps its humor and its values.
