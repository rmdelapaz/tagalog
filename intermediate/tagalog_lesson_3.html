Ang Focus sa Pakinabang — Benefactive Focus (i-)

Doing something for a beneficiary — the person you go to the trouble for

Lesson 2 ended on a teasing pair: binigyan (I gave Mom something — she received it) versus ibinili (I bought something for Mom — on her behalf). That second idea — acting for someone's benefit — is common enough in Tagalog to earn its own spotlight: benefactive focus, built with the prefix i-.

The system is the one you know. The affix and the ang marker agree; here ang lands on the beneficiary — the person who gains from the action — while the doer and the thing both drop to ng. A fourth spotlight on the same stage.

1. What Benefactive Focus Spotlights

The i- prefix aims the spotlight at the beneficiary: the one you do the action for. You cook for the family, buy for a friend, fetch water for Grandpa. That "for whom" is the beneficiary, and benefactive i- puts it in the ang spotlight.

  • ibili — buy for someone: Ibinili ko si Nanay ng gamot.
  • iluto — cook for someone: Iniluto ni Ana ang mga bata ng pansit.
  • ikuha — get/fetch for someone: Ikinuha ko ng tubig si Lolo.
  • idala — bring for someone: Idinala ko si Ana ng regalo.

Notice the beneficiary need not receive the thing at that moment — the point is that the action is done on their behalf. That's the whole difference from the recipient of Lesson 2.

2. The i- Verb Across Aspects

Benefactive i- sits on the front of the word, and the familiar aspect machinery — the -in- infix and reduplication — works inside, right after the i-. Here is bili → ibili (buy for) in all four aspects:

See how ibinili is just i- + binili (the completed object-focus form you already know). The i- rides in front while the aspect happens on the root. More common benefactive verbs:

3. Where ang Goes: the Beneficiary in Focus

Same swap, new target. In benefactive focus the ang spotlight lands on the beneficiary; the doer takes ng, and the thing also takes ng:

ang lands on the beneficiary

  • Ibinili ko si Nanay ng gamot. — I bought Mom medicine. BF
  • Iniluto ni Ana ang mga bata ng pansit. — Ana cooked the kids pancit.
  • Ikinuha ko ng tubig si Lolo. — I got Grandpa some water.

For personal names the spotlight marker is si (that's the ang-form for names), and the non-focus doer is ni. So si Nanay / si Lolo are the beneficiaries in focus — the thing bought or fetched stays back in ng.

4. Three Roles, Three Spotlights: for / to / it

This is the heart of the lesson. The same shopping trip can be told three ways depending on which role is the news. Watch the affix and the ang-phrase move together:

FocusSentenceang marks…Nuance
OF Object (-in)Binili ko ang gamot.the thing"I bought the medicine."
LF Locative (-an)Binigyan ko si Nanay ng gamot.the recipient"I gave Mom the medicine." (she receives it)
BF Benefactive (i-)Ibinili ko si Nanay ng gamot.the beneficiary"I bought the medicine for Mom." (on her behalf)
The give-to vs. do-for line, sharpened. Binigyan (LF, Lesson 2) is a hand-over: Mom ends up holding the medicine. Ibinili (BF) is a favor: I took on the buying for her — she may not even be there. If a real transfer reaches the person, think -an; if you're doing the work on their behalf, think i-.

5. The ipag- Variant

Some roots don't take a bare i- for the benefactive — they use the longer prefix ipag- instead. The meaning is the same ("do X for someone"); it's just the shape the root prefers. Luto is the classic one:

Aspecti- formipag- form
Infinitiveilutoipagluto
Completedinilutoipinagluto
Incompletedinilulutoipinagluluto
Contemplatedilulutoipagluluto
  • Ipinagluto ako ni Nanay ng pansit. — Mom cooked me pancit. (I'm the beneficiary — ako = ang-form)
  • Ipinagtimpla niya ng kape ang bisita. — She made the guest coffee.
Don't sweat the choice. For B1, just learn which verbs you hear with ipag- (luto, timpla, and a handful more) and use plain i- for the rest (bili, kuha, dala). Both are benefactive; both put ang on the beneficiary.

6. The Analytic Alternative: para kay / para sa

You don't have to reach for the benefactive affix. Tagalog also lets you keep an ordinary actor- or object-focus verb and tack on para kay / para sa ("for") — the analytic way to name a beneficiary:

Synthetic (benefactive affix)Analytic (para kay/sa)
Ibinili ko si Nanay ng gamot.Bumili ako ng gamot para kay Nanay.
Iniluto niya ang bata ng pansit.Nagluto siya ng pansit para sa bata.

Use para kay + a personal name (para kay Ana, para kay Nanay) and para sa + a common noun or pronoun (para sa bata, para sa iyo). The affix version spotlights the beneficiary; the para version keeps the doer or thing in focus and just adds the "for" phrase on the side. Both are correct — pick the one whose spotlight matches your conversation.

When each feels natural. The para kay/sa phrase is easy, flexible, and very common in speech — reach for it when you're unsure of a verb's benefactive form. The affixed form (ibinili, iniluto) sounds tighter and puts the person you helped squarely in the spotlight — great when they are the point of the sentence.

Where This Takes You

You now command four spotlights: actor, object, locative (give-to), and benefactive (do-for). The i- prefix (with its ipag- cousin) puts ang on the beneficiary, and para kay/sa gives you an easy analytic backup. Every one of these is the same ang↔ng machine from Lesson 1, aimed at a different role. Next we shift gears: instead of who is spotlighted, Lesson 4 asks who actually does the work — causative verbs (magpa-/ipa-), for when you have someone else do it.
